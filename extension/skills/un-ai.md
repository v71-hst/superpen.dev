# Un-AI

> Use when: designing anything from a blank frame, and whenever a design looks generic, templated or "AI-made" — or the user says so. Read this before the first screen of any new design, and again before showing it.

## The idea

Designs made by a machine look machine-made for one reason: every decision was the most likely one. The most likely font, the most likely gradient, the most likely three cards, the most likely headline. Each choice is defensible alone; together they produce a page that could belong to any product, and people now recognise that page instantly and trust it less.

The cure is not a different set of defaults. It is making decisions that come from *this* product, *this* audience and *this* content — and then committing to them more strongly than feels safe. A design with a point of view has something a person would have had to choose.

## The tells

If several of these are present, the design reads as generated. Know them so you can catch yourself.

**Colour and surface**
- A purple, indigo or violet accent, or a blue → purple (or pink → orange) gradient, chosen for no reason connected to the product.
- Gradient-filled headline text. Gradient buttons. A gradient on the page background *and* the cards.
- Blurred colour blobs or "aurora" glows behind the hero. Glassmorphism (frosted translucent cards) as the default surface.
- Dark navy/near-black page with neon accents and glowing borders, for a product that is not a nightclub.
- Every colour at the same medium saturation; pastel tints behind every icon.

**Shape and depth**
- Everything has the same large corner radius and the same soft, wide drop shadow.
- Cards inside cards inside a rounded section.
- An icon in a tinted rounded square at the top of every card.
- Pills everywhere: pill buttons, pill badges, pill tabs, a pill "✨ New feature" announcement above the headline.

**Layout**
- The standard page, in the standard order: centred hero with badge, two-line headline, grey subtitle, two buttons → logo strip → three feature cards in a row → alternating image/text → three pricing tiers with the middle one raised → testimonial cards → big gradient call-to-action → four-column footer.
- Perfect symmetry and everything centred. Three of everything. All cards the same size, in an even grid, with the same amount of text.
- A "bento grid" used because it is fashionable rather than because the content has that shape.
- Dashboards: four stat cards with tiny green "+12.5%" labels, then a line chart, then a table — whatever the product is.

**Type**
- Inter (or the system default) at 400 and 700, nothing else, for a brand that should have a voice.
- Headline huge and bold, then everything else the same small grey.
- Emoji used as icons or bullets. Sparkles (✨), rockets (🚀) and lightning bolts for "AI", "fast" and "new".

**Words**
- Headlines made of two commands: "Build faster. Ship smarter." Or "The future of X is here." Or "X, reimagined."
- Seamless, effortless, elevate, unlock, supercharge, empower, streamline, cutting-edge, all-in-one, next-level.
- Invented proof: "Trusted by 10,000+ teams", "99.9% uptime", five-star testimonials from "Sarah J., Product Manager", fake company logos.
- Feature cards titled "Lightning Fast", "Secure by Default", "Built for Scale", each with exactly two lines of description.
- John Doe, Jane Smith, Acme Inc., lorem ipsum, $9 / $29 / $99.

**Content**
- Decoration standing in for content: abstract shapes where a picture of the actual product should be.
- Perfectly even fake data: every name the same length, every number round, every chart rising smoothly up and to the right.

One or two of these do not condemn a design — a pill button is just a button. It is the *accumulation*, and above all the absence of any choice that is not on this list, that gives it away.

## What to do instead

1. **Start from the content, not the template.** Before drawing: what does this product actually have to show? The real items, real numbers, real names, the real screenshot. Let the amount and shape of the content decide the layout. If there are two features, show two. If one matters most, give it most of the page.
2. **Write down a point of view in one line.** "A workshop tool: dense, flat, monospace numbers, one orange." "A kids' game: chunky, bouncy, thick outlines, loud." "An editorial product: big serif, lots of white, hairlines, no cards." Every later decision is checked against that line. No line → generic result.
3. **Take the colour from somewhere.** The product's world, its existing brand, a photograph, a material, a place, a period. One accent with a reason beats a fashionable gradient. If the brief gives nothing, choose something *not* purple-blue and commit: a warm off-white with ink and one saturated spot; a deep green; black, white and a single red.
4. **Choose type with character, and use its range.** One family with a voice (see the typography skill for names), using contrast in size and weight on purpose: something very large next to something small; a light weight where bold is expected, or the reverse.
5. **Break the symmetry once.** Left-align the hero. Make one card twice the size. Let a picture run off the edge. Put the number where the headline would be. An uneven layout that follows the content looks designed; a perfectly even one looks produced.
6. **Pick one way of making surfaces and hold it.** Flat with hairlines. *Or* soft shadows. *Or* solid colour blocks. *Or* thick outlines. Not all of them, and not "rounded card with soft shadow" by reflex. Sharp corners are allowed.
7. **Use fewer boxes.** Much of what is in a card can sit directly on the page, separated by space and a rule. Reserve containers for things that are objects.
8. **Show the real thing.** A screenshot, a product photo, the actual game art, a real chart with uneven data, an honest diagram. If there is no imagery, strong type and space are better than abstract blobs.
9. **Write like a person who knows the product.** Say what it does, concretely, with the real number if there is one. No invented statistics, customers or quotes — leave a clearly marked gap for the user to fill instead. (See the writing skill.)
10. **Make specific placeholder content.** Names of different lengths and origins, prices that are not .99, a list with one awkwardly long item, a chart with a dip.
11. **One bold decision per screen.** A very large number. A full-bleed colour. An unusual grid. A single enormous word. Then keep everything else quiet so it lands.
12. **Subtract the ornaments.** Remove the badge above the headline, the gradient on the text, the glow, the icon tiles, the second button. See what is left. Usually it is better; add back only what is missed.

## The swap test

Take a screenshot and imagine replacing the logo and product name with a competitor's — or with a product from a different industry altogether. If nothing else would need to change, the design has no point of view yet. Find the three things that would have to change if it were truly about this product, and make those three things stronger.

## Game UI

The same disease has a different face in games: clean rounded web-style cards with soft shadows and a sans-serif, dropped onto a game that has its own world. Game UI should look as if it was made *of* the game — its materials, shapes, colours and attitude. A cartoon simulator wants thick outlines, chunky bevels, saturated colour, bouncy shapes; a military shooter wants stencils, hard angles, muted tones, tight grids; a cosy game wants soft hand-drawn edges and warm paper. Take cues from the game's own art before drawing a single panel, and prefer its typeface and icon style over generic ones.

## But stay useful

- Un-AI does not mean strange. The interface still has to be clear, accessible and consistent; conventions that help people (a search field that looks like one, a back arrow at top left) stay.
- The user's own brand, references and existing file come first. If their brand is purple, purple is right. If they ask for a gradient or a glass panel, make an excellent one. This skill is about not choosing those things *by default*.
- Match what exists: adding one "bold" screen to a calm, established product is worse than fitting in.
- Do not explain the design with a manifesto. Make the choices and state them in one line.

## In Superpencil

- Before designing: `get_variables`, `list_components`, and a `screenshot` of existing frames — the file's own style is the first source of specificity. Read `note`/`context` layers for the brief.
- Write the one-line point of view into a `context` note beside the first frame, so later sessions keep to it.
- Set the palette and type as variables first (`set_variables`), from the point of view — not from habit.
- Use real assets when the user has them (image fills by relative path; `import_html` for an existing site's content; `insert_svg` or AI Pen for a mark) instead of abstract decoration.
- `search_nodes` for give-away words and filler ("seamless", "lorem", "John Doe", "Acme") before finishing.
- Compare with a thumbnail (`screenshot`, max_size ~300): does it look like a hundred other pages at that size?

## Check before you finish

- [ ] You can state the design's point of view in one line, and it is specific to this product.
- [ ] The accent colour and the typeface each have a reason you can say out loud.
- [ ] Swap test passed: at least three things would have to change for another product.
- [ ] No more than two items from the list of tells remain, and each one is there on purpose.
- [ ] No invented statistics, testimonials, logos or customer names.
- [ ] Not everything is centred; not everything comes in threes; not every surface is a rounded shadowed card.
- [ ] There is one bold decision on the screen, and the rest is quiet.
- [ ] The words say what the product does in plain language.

## Common mistakes

- Replacing one template with another (swapping purple gradients for the "beige editorial serif" look on everything).
- Being different at the cost of being usable.
- Adding quirks on top of a generic structure instead of rethinking the structure from the content.
- Ignoring the user's existing brand in order to be original.
- Timid commitment: a point of view applied at 20% strength reads as an accident.
